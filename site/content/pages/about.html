About One More Tree

We started with 200 trees and a spreadsheet.

In 2019 a group of six — two botanists, a hydrologist, a drone pilot and two people who simply wanted to plant something — put 200 native saplings into a burnt hillside above Guadix. That winter, 41 of them died.

What survived was a method. We learned that a Mediterranean forest is not built on planting day; it is built over five dry summers, by people who live close enough to walk to the plot with a watering can. Everything One More Tree does today is a formalisation of what those 159 surviving trees taught us.

We are a small organisation by design. We would rather restore 38 plots properly and publish what went wrong than claim a million trees we never counted.

Our method, in four steps

Restoration is a sequence. Doing the steps out of order is the most common reason a planting project fails quietly three years later.

STEP 01

Read the land first

Soil depth, aspect, rainfall history, fire record and existing seed bank. Six weeks of survey before a single sapling is ordered — because the wrong tree in the right place is still the wrong tree.

STEP 02

Source seed locally

Seed from the same watershed and altitude band. A tree grown from Portuguese seed may survive in Granada — but the insects, fungi and birds around it never evolved with it.

STEP 03

Plant with the neighbours

Volunteers and local guardians do the planting, guided by our botanists. A plot planted by the people who will see it every week gets looked after.

STEP 04

Stay for five summers

A paid steward, a watering schedule and a monitoring contract for five years. Then we publish the survival rate — whatever it turns out to be.

What makes these forests different

Four decisions distinguish a plot we manage from a conventional plantation. They are not glamorous, and they are the reason our survival numbers look the way they do.

Mixed species, never a monoculture

A plot carries between four and nine species, matched to micro-variations in soil and water. If a pest or a dry decade takes one, the forest does not fail as a whole.

Below-ground carbon is the real prize

Mediterranean dryland forest stores a remarkable share of its carbon in deep root systems rather than trunks. Those roots also survive brushfire — which is why this carbon stays locked for centuries, and why we measure soil carbon, not just trunk volume.

Water is designed in, not added later

Micro-catchments and crescent-shaped earthworks around each seedling capture early-morning condensation and slope run-off. On steep terrain this alone lifts first-summer survival by a third.

Every plot has a named guardian

Not a contractor passing through, but a resident with a five-year agreement and a paid stipend. Stewardship is the most underfunded part of reforestation and the only part that determines whether a forest exists in 2050.

How we got here

2019

200 saplings above Guadix. 41 lost. The stewardship model is written as a result.

2021

First regional seed bank opens. Twenty-one native species catalogued by watershed and altitude.

2023

Drone monitoring goes fleet-wide. NDVI mapping turns watering from a schedule into a response.

2026

38 sites, 612 hectares, 148,500 trees. First self-seeding recorded at four plots.

Who we are

Eleven full-time staff across botany, hydrology, remote sensing and field operations, plus 2,400 volunteers who have planted with us since 2019. Our field team is based in Granada; our monitoring lab is in Valencia.

Our commitments

  • No figure is published that we cannot trace to a plot and a date
  • Survival rates are reported including trees that died
  • 80% of every donation goes to field work; 20% to monitoring and administration
  • No offsetting claims sold before the carbon is measured
