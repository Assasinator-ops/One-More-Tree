Planting weeks run between October and March, when the soil is cool and the rain does most of the watering. No experience needed — just six hours of patience on uneven ground.

What a planting week actually looks like

Volunteers arrive on Sunday evening, work Monday to Saturday, and leave having put several hundred trees into ground they can name. Groups are capped at twenty so everyone works in a pair with a trained guardian.

  • Day 1 — site briefing, technique, tool safety and a short walk over the plot you will be planting.
  • Days 2–5 — planting in pairs, six hours a day. Work stops in the heat of the early afternoon.
  • Day 6 — micro-catchments, mulch and the first drone survey of what you planted.
  • After the week — you receive the plot's monitoring data twice a year, so you can watch it survive.

Accommodation is a shared rural hostel, included. Travel to Granada is not covered. Under-18s are welcome with a guardian.

What to bring

  • Sturdy boots (essential)
  • Work gloves and sun hat
  • Water bottle — at least 2 litres
  • Layers — cold mornings, warm afternoons
  • Rain shell
  • Tools and saplings are provided

Not everyone plants

Planting weeks need muscle, but our sites need three other kinds of volunteer just as badly.

Local stewards

Live within an hour of a plot? Stewardship is a paid commitment across the whole summer — watering, weeding and reporting. There is a waiting list and we would rather train a neighbour than hire a contractor.

Survey volunteers

Ground transects are counted by hand, and hands are the bottleneck. If you can identify Mediterranean tree species or are willing to learn, two days per season makes our survival figures stronger.

Skills & pro bono

Hydrologists, GIS specialists, drone pilots, translators and data engineers have all saved us months. If your day job overlaps with our problem list, we would like to hear from you.
